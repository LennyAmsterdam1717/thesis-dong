TI: Machine Economic Agency: Risk-First Longitudinal Evaluation of Financial LLM Agents
AB: This preprint introduces machine economic agency: a framing for financial AI systems whose decisions are embedded in economic feedback loops. The
    work instantiates the idea through WisdomBench-Finance, a risk-first longitudinal evaluation protocol for financial LLM agents. Each decision is
    recorded as a causal tuple of visible evidence, pre-policy intent, risk-gate reasons, final action, execution metadata, audit trace, and later
    same-run market outcomes. On an audit-valid paper-market runtime spanning 64,244 decisions and 3,220 cycles across 20 assets, risk governance
    changes behavior materially: 30.27% of rows touch at least one risk gate and 10,971 non-neutral candidate actions are suppressed to neutral.
    Same-run realization joins show a horizon-dependent profile: the active cohort is negative at 1d but positive at 3d and 5d. These results are
    presented as audit and benchmark evidence, not as alpha dominance, live-money robustness, or investment advice.

    traditional advisory channels. This research provides practical guidance for financial professionals and plan sponsors seeking to identify which
    employee segments are most receptive to AI‐powered retirement planning tools, with implications for reaching participants who express interest but

AB: Purpose – Banks are moving generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) chatbots from answering routine queries to giving personalized financial
    guidance. This shift raises a central question: when will customers trust, and act on, advice from a machine that can be wrong? Drawing on the
    stimulus–organism–response (SOR) framework, this conceptual paper develops a model of how GenAI advisory chatbots shape customers' willingness to
    accept their advice. Design/methodology/approach – The paper integrates recent (2025) evidence from marketing, information systems, finance and
    human–computer interaction research to build a theory-driven model and a set of testable propositions. Findings – The model proposes two trust
    routes. Perceived advice quality builds cognitive trust (confidence in the chatbot's competence), and perceived empathy builds emotional trust (a
    feeling of security and care). Both forms of trust increase advice acceptance. The availability of a human handoff, meaning the option to escalate
    to a human adviser, is proposed to strengthen both routes by acting as a safety net. Six propositions formalize these relationships. Research
    limitations/implications – The propositions require empirical testing. The paper sets out a scenario-based experimental design, a complementary
    field survey and recommended measures for doing so. Practical implications – For banks, chatbot accuracy alone is not enough. Customers also need
    to feel understood, and they need a visible route to a human when stakes are high. Designing for calibrated trust, rather than maximum trust,
    protects both customers and banks in a regulated industry. Originality/value – The paper is one of the few to separate cognitive and emotional
    trust in generative advisory chatbots in banking, and it positions human handoff as a design lever that shapes whether trust turns into action.
    Keywords: generative AI; chatbots; banking; financial advice; cognitive trust; emotional trust; human handoff; stimulus–organism–response;
    conceptual paper

    Elicitation

    using software, especially in sensitive domains. Yet, eliciting ERs via interviews, workshops, and focus groups is costly and hard to scale or
    repeat. We study whether simulated focus groups, moderated discussions among user personas generated and played by large language models (LLMs),
    can augment early ER elicitation. Across three domains (mental‑health journaling, personal finance, and fitness), we compare one‑shot and
    iterative single‑model pipelines with iterative pipelines that use two or three different LLMs, and we benchmark against two human baselines: a

    requirements, EGM contributes clearer and more feasible ones, and human focus groups provide more natural phrasing. Overall, the results support
    an augmentation‑oriented workflow: use multi‑LLM simulation to broaden candidate ERs, structured modeling to organize them, and human engagement
    to ground decisions.

AB: Shariah-compliant chatbots are increasingly used to explain Islamic financial products such as Takaful, yet their interfaces are typically
    modelled on generic chat applications and evaluated only after release through end-user questionnaires. This practice is problematic for
    marginalized users with uneven financial, digital and language literacy, who are the least able to articulate why an interface fails, and in a
    faith-based domain a failure of clarity is also a failure of trust. Usability evaluation alone cannot judge whether content, tone and branding
    suit the Shariah context, while domain review alone cannot diagnose interaction problems. To address this gap, this study proposes and applies a
    dual-expert review that combines Takaful domain experts from the Malaysian Takaful Association (MTA) with academic UI/UX experts in the formative
    evaluation of SCAI, a bilingual mobile conversational agent. Domain experts were engaged through a focus group after a prototype demonstration,
    while UI/UX experts tested the prototype independently and were interviewed with an eleven-item protocol mapped to QUIS, USE and Chatbot Usability
    Scale (BUS) constructs. Thematic and root-cause analysis yielded four themes: visual design and branding, navigation and interface interaction,
    language consistency and accessibility, and conversational interaction. Each theme was traced to implemented refinements through a
    construct-mapped traceability matrix and triangulated against end-user ratings of the refined application (n = 33). The two expert groups surfaced
    complementary rather than redundant issues: domain experts judged legitimacy, whereas UI/UX experts judged operability. The study contributes four
    design principles for Shariah-sensitive conversational interfaces, namely legitimacy cues, provenance transparency, linguistic completeness and
    expectation setting, and argues that in this domain usability is part of how users judge Shariah assurance rather than a cosmetic concern.

TI: “What Could Possibly Go Wrong?”: A Consumer‐Centric Approach to Exploring Vulnerability and Algorithmic Bias in AI ‐Driven Financial Planning
AB: ABSTRACT With the accessibility of generative AI, the uptake of consumers using it for their financial planning queries and needs has
    exponentially increased. However, algorithmic bias in AI‐driven financial planning poses significant challenges, particularly to consumers
    experiencing vulnerabilities as it can cause further marginalization by the inherent biases in the algorithms that drive decision‐making
    processes. The aim of this research is to determine the extent to which biases manifest in the output of AI models and explore whether consumer
    vulnerabilities are considered in its recommendations. Therefore, we employ an experimental research design by generating prompts that are based
    on a financial need with varying states of vulnerability. Through a content and discourse analysis, a comparison of three AI tools, namely
    Perplexity, ChatGPT, and Claude, is provided. Our findings reveal that while biases were not explicitly pronounced in the output, the
    vulnerabilities presented were not robustly considered. Perplexity often referred the user to seek professional advice but lacked detail; ChatGPT
    avoided implicit bias but under‐addressed vulnerabilities, and Claude gave thorough recommendations that required self‐guided financial planning.
    Furthermore, we found that at face value, the model outputs were similar, but upon analysis, there were variations in the recommendations provided
    which had long‐term implications for consumers experiencing vulnerabilities. This paper presents key considerations for consumers engaging with AI
    in the context of financial planning. Understanding how the AI models respond to and account for consumers experiencing vulnerability is crucial
    for advancing the way in which AI is applied as an avenue for financial advice and how it can be used for a more inclusive financial landscape.
