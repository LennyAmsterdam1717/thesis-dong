TI: Damage Predicts Recovery: When Calibration Data Matters in Compressing Financial LLMs
AB: Post-training quantization and pruning rely on a small calibration corpus. Whether specialized domains such as finance require domain-matched
    calibration data remains unsettled. We argue that the answer depends on the task-level damage caused by compression rather than on domain
    mismatch. If compression preserves the target capability, changing the calibration corpus has little effect. If compression causes large losses,
    task-formatted calibration can recover part of the loss. We test this hypothesis across two model families, six compression configurations, three
    token-matched calibration corpora, and ten financial classification and numerical question-answering tasks. The results support this hypothesis.
    Quantization largely preserves task performance, and calibration choice has little effect in this case. Pruning reduces numerical QA accuracy by
    over 40 points. In these damaged settings, another generic corpus does not help, while FinMix, a mixture of financial task examples, recovers a
    large part of the loss. The link between damage and recovery holds across model families and scales. These findings support a practical rule.
    Measure task-specific compression damage first, and construct specialized calibration data only when the damage is large.

TI: Hallucination In Large Language Models: A Review Of Causes, Taxonomy, Detection Methods And Mitigation Strategies
AB: Large Language Models (LLMs) generate human-like text, answer questions and summarize information, but they sometimes produce incorrect,
    misleading or fabricated content while presenting it as fact. This phenomenon, known as hallucination, reduces the accuracy, trustworthiness and
    safety of generative AI systems, particularly in education, healthcare, law and finance. This paper reviews LLM hallucination in a structured way.
    It explains causes arising from training data, model architecture and training objectives, decoding and missing context, and presents a taxonomy
    that distinguishes factuality from faithfulness errors. Benchmarks that measure truthfulness and hallucination rates are discussed. Detection
    methods are reviewed, including self-consistency checks across sampled answers, uncertainty measures such as semantic entropy and fine-grained
    atomic fact verification. Mitigation strategies are examined across the model life cycle: data curation, alignment with human feedback,
    retrieval-augmented generation, prompting for verification and abstention when uncertain. A layered hallucination-aware LLM application
    architecture is proposed, along with research gaps, objectives and a comparison of methods. The review concludes that hallucination cannot be
    eliminated entirely but can be detected, reduced and communicated so that users can rely on LLM outputs appropriately.

    the underlying causes of hallucination identified in prior work, review representative techniques at each intervention point – including
    confidence based decoding constraints, instruction-tuning for faithfulness, hybrid sparse-dense retrieval with rank fusion, cross-encoder

    category offers. We further examine evaluation practices and benchmarks used to measure hallucination and identify a set of open problems,
    including the absence of standardized crosscategory benchmarks, the tension between validation strictness and unnecessary refusal, and the limited
    maturity of hallucination mitigation for multi-hop and cross document reasoning. We conclude with directions for future work that combines
    lightweight decoding-time safeguards with heavier, verification-based agentic pipelines depending on application risk.

AB: This paper addresses a class of failure modes in large language model safety systems that are structurally distinct from the problem those systems
    are designed to prevent. The paper begins from an unambiguous premise: efforts to make LLMs safer are necessary, consequential, and deserving of
    continued investment. It then argues that some implementations produce specific, identifiable costs that fall disproportionately on the users

    users who generate these failure modes from the class the interventions were designed to protect — a distinction the two classes' behavioral
