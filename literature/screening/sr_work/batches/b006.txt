TI: Users perceptions of chatbot bullshitting
AB: Context Since its original formulation, the Turing Test has served as a benchmark for evaluating machine intelligence based on linguistic behavior
    (Turing, 1950). Rather than assessing internal reasoning or problem-solving capabilities, the test focuses on whether a machine can produce
    language indistinguishable from that of a human in a text-based conversation. This emphasis on linguistic fluency as an indicator for intelligence
    reflects a long-standing human tendency to infer cognitive abilities from conversational competence. In practice, people are sensitive to
    surface-level linguistic cues such as coherence, grammaticality, and contextual appropriateness, often using them to evaluate the mental states or
    intelligence of others (Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009). As such, systems like large language models (LLMs), which produce fluent and natural-sounding
    language, are often perceived as more intelligent, even in the absence of genuine understanding or reasoning (Moussawi, Koufaris, & Benbunan-Fich,
    2020). This behavior aligns with findings in social psychology and cognitive science, where language fluency is known to activate the fluency
    heuristic, leading people to equate ease of processing with competence and truth (Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009). The Turing Test thus highlights a
    key aspect of human behavior: the strong influence of communicative performance on attributions of intelligence, which remains relevant, but also
    potentially problematic, in the evaluation of today’s conversational AI systems. According to previous study participants presented a variety of
    expectations when discussing their interactions with chatbots. These expectations extended beyond the functional capabilities of the chatbot to
    encompass emotional and experiential aspects. User satisfaction seemed less tied to the actual accuracy or performance of the chatbot and more to
    the perception of its intelligence and competence. This phenomenon might be described as a form of "self-deception" or cognitive bias, where
    perceived competence outweighs actual reliability. The linguistic fluency of large language models (LLMs) often leads users to perceive them as
    more competent (Wester, de Jong, Pohl, & van Berkel, 2024). Their ability to produce coherent, context-sensitive, and persuasive language fosters
    the impression of cognitive depth and intentionality. However, this surface-level fluency masks a fundamental epistemic limitation: LLMs do not
    possess an understanding of the truth or a commitment to factual accuracy. Their responses are generated based on probabilistic associations in
    language, not on grounded knowledge or communicative intent. This phenomenon aligns with Harry Frankfurt’s (2005) Theory of Bullshit, in which he
    distinguishes bullshit from lying. Whereas liars deliberately distort known truths, bullshitters are indifferent to the truth and focus instead on
    producing statements that seem plausible or convincing. LLMs operate in a similar manner generating text that mimics coherent discourse without
    any regard for the veracity of the information presented. Their goal, implicitly, is not deception but fluency; they prioritize plausible language
    over factual correctness. This lack of epistemic commitment complicates human evaluations of LLM output, as users may mistake fluency for
    credibility, thereby overestimating the system’s intelligence and reliability. As such, LLMs embody a computational form of "bullshit": not by
    intention, but by design. Some psychological tendencies help explain why bullshit, as conceptualized by (Frankfurt, 2005), can be effective in
    social and human-computer interactions. The fluency heuristic, for instance, suggests that individuals judge statements as more truthful and
    reliable when they are articulated clearly and effortlessly (Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009). Language that is easy to process increases perceived
    credibility, even in the absence of evidence. This is compounded by authority bias, where people are more likely to accept information from
    sources that appear competent or confident, often equating fluency with expertise (Milgram, 1974; Kelley, 1973). Additionally, the Barnum effect,
    where individuals find vague, general, but positively framed statements personally meaningful, further enhances the persuasive impact of fluent
    but unsubstantiated language (Forer, 1949). When applied to interactions with large language models (LLMs), these biases help explain why users
    often interpret fluent, confidently presented responses as intelligent, accurate, and even personalized, despite the absence of actual
    understanding or epistemic commitment from the system. LLMs like ChatGPT, Bard, and other generative AI models do not have intrinsic knowledge.
    They predict the most statistically likely sequence of words based on their training data. This means that: • They do not "lie" intentionally
    (since they lack intent). • They do not verify truthfulness. They only aim for coherence and plausibility. • Their goal is to produce responses
    that sound right rather than be right. This aligns with Frankfurt’s definition of bullshit: LLMs are not concerned with truth but rather with the
    generation of plausible, fluent text. Language fluency refers to the perceived smoothness, coherence, and naturalness of linguistic output in
    human or artificial communication (McNamara, Crossley, & Mccarthy, 2010; Pitler & Nenkova, 2008). In the context of conversational agents, fluency
    encompasses grammatical accuracy, lexical appropriateness, discourse coherence, and prosodic or stylistic naturalness (Clark, Asli, & Smith,
    2019). Large Language Model (LLM)-based chatbots, such as those powered by transformer architectures (e.g., GPT-4), typically generate highly
    fluent outputs due to their data-driven ability to model human-like language across diverse contexts (OpenAI, 2023; (Brown, Mann, Ryder, &

TI: Artificial Intelligence - Large Language Models (LLMs) and Agentic AI in Clinical Decision Support in Outpatient, General Practice and Acute
    Settings: A Systematic Review of Diagnostic Accuracy, Comparative Efficacy, Clinical Outcomes and Implementation Factors
AB: Abstract Background : Large language models (LLMs) and agentic artificial intelligence (AI) systems represent a notable shift in clinical decision
    support systems (CDSS), offering potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy, triage efficiency, and clinical decision-making, in outpatient, general
    practice as well as acute care settings. Despite rapid technological advancement and increasing deployment, systematic evidence synthesis on their
    diagnostic accuracy, comparative efficacy, clinical outcomes, and implementation factors remains limited. This systematic review aimed to
    comprehensively evaluate the current evidence on LLM-based CDSS in general practice or outpatient, as well as acute and emergency settings.
    Methods: We conducted a systematic review following PRISMA 2020 guidelines. We searched 11 databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, Science Direct, Web of
    Science, IEEE Xplore, ACM, Computer Science Journals, Google Scholar, CINAHL, Academic Search Ultimate, APA PsychInfo, and Elicit AI) from January
    2021 to December 2026. Inclusion criteria encompassed studies evaluating generative AI/LLMs and agentic AI in CDSS for general practice,
    outpatient, acute, or emergency settings, reporting original data on at least one primary outcome domain. Two independent reviewers conducted
    title/abstract screening and full-text eligibility assessment. Quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Data were
    extracted using a standardized framework and synthesized thematically across four domains: diagnostic accuracy, comparative efficacy, clinical
    outcomes, and implementation factors. Results: From 2,402 identified records, 50 studies met all inclusion criteria following deduplication and
    two-stage screening (inter-rater κ = 0.78-0.85). Studies were published between 2021 and 2026, with 68% published in 2025 alone, reflecting the
    rapid emergence of advanced LLMs in clinical evaluations. Studies evaluated LLMs including GPT-4/4o, GPT-3.5, Claude 3 (Opus/Sonnet/Haiku), Gemini
    1.5 Pro/2.5 Flash, DeepSeek-R1, Llama 3, Med-PaLM 2, Doubao, Qwen, and Mistral, across emergency care (n=18, 36%), primary/general care (n=22,
    44%), virtual and simulated environments (n=5, 10%), and mixed settings (n=5, 10%). Study designs included quantitative descriptive studies,
    encompassing benchmarks and vignettes, (n=28, 56%), retrospective observational studies (n=5, 10%), prospective observational studies (n=4, 8%),
    randomised controlled trials (n=4, 8%), qualitative studies (n=3, 6%), cross-sectional studies (n=5, 10%), and mixed-methods (n=1, 2%). LLMs
    demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy in structured vignettes and controlled settings, with advanced models (o1, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet)
    achieving ≥90% accuracy in final diagnoses in emergency internal medicine and matching specialist-level performance in cardiovascular emergencies.
    In the largest prospective triage study to date, screening 6,657 patients across four emergency departments, ChatGPT-4o outperformed triage staff
    overall (Cohen's κ = 0.833 vs 0.782; F1 = 0.897 vs 0.863), though performance declined for complex, high-acuity presentations requiring yellow and
    red zone triage (AUC = 0.75-0.78). Beyond struggle with complex contextual reasoning, LLMs exhibited clinically important weaknesses across
    atypical presentations, autonomous safety planning in psychiatric triage, and real-world clinical recommendation tasks. For example, in a study
    evaluating accuracy in real, unstructured clinical environment, spanning 10,000 ED visits, GPT-4 accuracy fell 8-24% below resident physicians on
    clinical recommendation tasks. Human-AI collaborative models consistently outperformed both independent AI and unassisted clinicians. A large
    vignette study generating 40,762 diagnoses across 2,133 cases demonstrated that clinician-LLM collectives outperformed all individual groups,
    driven by complementary error profiles. A position augmented by a real-world deployment study across 15 Kenyan primary care clinics (n = 39,849
    visits), where LLM-based decision support reduced diagnostic errors by 16% and treatment errors by 13%. Similarly, Retrieval-augmented Generation
    (RAG) significantly improved guideline concordance (95.5% vs 62-74% for junior clinicians) and prescribing error detection by 1.5-fold over
    unaided pharmacists across 16 clinical specialties. Implementation findings identified automation bias (15-35% of cases), trust calibration
    deficits, workflow integration challenges, and regulatory uncertainty as critical barriers, with explainable AI and human-in-the-loop designs as
    key facilitators. Conclusions: LLMs and agentic AI CDSS show substantial promise as adjunctive co-pilot tools capable of improving diagnostic
    accuracy and reducing clinical errors across varied acuity and resource settings. However, significant vulnerabilities in contextual reasoning,
    autonomous safety planning, and unaided real world performance currently preclude their use as autonomous decision-makers. Future integration must
    prioritise retrieval-augmented generation, clinician oversight, explainability, and rigorous real-world prospective evaluation with

TI: More is not better: Visual uncertainty cues and the fragility of trust calibration in LLM-assisted decision making
AB: As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into decision-support systems, understanding how users interpret uncertainty
    expressions is critical for responsible deployment. Prior work has shown that verbal hedging (e.g., “I'm not sure, but …”) can reduce overreliance
    on incorrect outputs. We examine whether combining verbal hedging with visual confidence indicators improves trust calibration in a pre-registered
    2 × 2 × 2 mixed-design experiment ( N = 495 ). Response accuracy was the dominant driver of trust and reliance across all conditions. Visual
    confidence indicators improved users’ subjective sensitivity to accuracy, reflected in higher per-participant discrimination indices and steeper
    confidence-accuracy slopes, yet simultaneously amplified behavioral agreement with incorrect outputs. Verbal hedging reduced agreement and
    satisfaction without improving discrimination. Combining both cues produced the highest overreliance on incorrect responses alongside the lowest
    rates of independent source verification. These findings reveal a dissociation between subjective and behavioral calibration as users can become
    more sensitive to accuracy signals while simultaneously making worse decisions. Simply adding more uncertainty cues does not produce more
    trustworthy or interpretable LLM systems; what matters is whether signals translate into behavior-relevant calibration.

### R00199 | 2026 | article/None | Lang: en
TI: Why we believe chatbots: trust calibration as a design problem
AB: Chatbots powered by large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT and Claude, answer questions quickly and fluently, yet they reveal little about
    how their answers are produced or what evidence supports them. Users tend to trust these systems based on surface qualities such as fluency,
    confidence, and speed. This trust is often miscalibrated, meaning that it does not match what the systems can actually do. Some users overtrust
    answers they cannot verify. Others distrust the systems and disengage. Large surveys show that many people rely on AI daily while saying they do
    not trust it. This article treats miscalibration as a design problem and develops a framework for recalibrating user trust. We first define trust
    in chatbots by connecting psychological research on trust, communication research on credibility, and human factors research on trust in
    automation. We then introduce a user typology built on two dimensions, the ability to verify chatbot outputs and the motivation to do so, which
    together predict how a given user will miscalibrate. On this basis, we synthesize two families of design intervention. Interpretability
    affordances, such as source citations and uncertainty cues, make evaluation possible. Engagement mechanisms, such as cooling-off periods and
    dialogue scaffolds, make evaluation happen by building it into the interaction. Following the Swiss cheese model of layered defense, we argue that
    these interventions cover one another's gaps, and we state eight testable propositions that link each intervention to the users it serves. We
    close by discussing AI literacy as the slowest but most durable layer of defense.

TI: Addressing Uncertainty in LLM Outputs for Trust Calibration Through Visualization and User Interface Design
AB: Large language models (LLMs) are becoming ubiquitous in knowledge work. However, the uncertainty inherent to LLM summary generation limits the
    efficacy of human-machine teaming, especially when users are unable to properly calibrate their trust in automation. Visual conventions for
    signifying uncertainty and interface design strategies for engaging users are needed to realize the full potential of LLMs. We report on an

    intelligence analysis context. First, we provide and evaluate eight potential visual conventions for representing uncertainty in LLM summaries.
    Second, we describe a framework for uncertainty specific to LLM technology. Third, we specify 10 features for a proposed LLM validation system —
    the Multiple Agent Validation System (MAVS) — that utilizes the visual conventions, the framework, and three virtual agents to aid in language
    analysis. Fourth, we provide and describe four MAVS prototypes, one as an interactive simulation interface and the others as narrative interface
    videos. All four utilize a language analysis scenario to educate users on the potential of LLM technology in human-machine teams. To demonstrate
    applicability of the contributions beyond intelligence analysis, we also consider LLM-derived uncertainty in clinical decision-making in medicine
    and in climate forecasting. Ultimately, this investigation makes a case for the importance of visual and interface design in shaping the
    development of LLM technology.

TI: LLM Confidence Paradox
AB: This research project empirically investigates the "Confidence Calibration Paradox," a core tension in designing trustworthy AI. The paradox
    arises because Large Language Models (LLMs) often express themselves with an assertive linguistic style that is misaligned with their internal
    certainty (a "confidence miscalibration"), yet human users are known to be more persuaded by confident communication. We question whether
    designing AIs to be epistemically honest (transparently showing uncertainty) might paradoxically reduce user trust, while AIs designed for social
    effectiveness (using confident language) risk propagating misinformation. To address this, we will conduct a pre-registered series of four
    iterative online experiments. These studies will causally estimate the effects of manipulating LLM linguistic assertiveness (e.g., hedged vs.
    assertive language) and numerical confidence disclosure (showing or hiding the AI's internal self-certainty score) on user Trust, Persuasion, and
    Behavioral Intentions. The experiments are designed to: Isolate the baseline effect of linguistic assertiveness alone. Determine the main causal
    effect of disclosing the AI's numerical confidence score. Test the interaction between linguistic assertiveness and numerical disclosure to see
    how users respond to (mis)aligned signals. Evaluate an alternative interface design (prompting users to estimate AI confidence) as a potential
    strategy for improving user calibration. Expected Outcomes: This research program will provide a causal map of how users respond to different AI
    confidence communication strategies. The findings will offer direct evidence on the nature of the Confidence Calibration Paradox and yield
    actionable design guidelines for building LLM interfaces that foster more appropriately calibrated user trust. We also expect to identify
    individual characteristics (e.g., numeracy) that may make users more or less susceptible to being influenced by miscalibrated AI confidence
    expressions.

AB: The studyLarge language models are now consulted for medical symptoms, financial decisions, and legal questions. Users have no easy way to tell
    when an answer is right and when it is confidently wrong. This study examines what actually drives trust in commercial LLMs, and where that trust
    goes wrong.The central question is whether users trust AI because it is accurate, or because it sounds sure of itself. The answer, from 1,000
    interactions across six commercial models and twelve query domains, is that confidence and accuracy are almost inseparable in practice. Confidence
    correlates with accuracy at r = 0.857. Users respond to confidence, but confidence carries real information about accuracy. The trouble is that
    users cannot tell the 86 per cent of cases where confidence tracks accuracy from the 14 per cent where it diverges.The manuscript reports three
    findings that reframe the problem. First, over-trust is driven more by inaccuracy than by confidence: the correlation between confidence and the
    trust gap is only −0.199, while the correlation between accuracy and the trust gap is −0.466. Second, the trust gap shrinks monotonically as
    accuracy rises, from +2.33 in the lowest accuracy decile to −0.39 in the highest. Third, over-trust is largest in medium-stakes domains such as
    opinion-based queries and current events (+1.93), where correctness is ambiguous and verification is difficult, not in high-stakes medical,
    financial, or legal domains (+0.83), where users show more caution.The datasetThe dataset contains 1,000 human–AI interactions across six
    commercial LLMs (Claude, GPT-4, Gemini, Llama, Mistral, ChatGPT-3.5) and twelve query domains. Each row is one interaction.Response properties.
    The model that generated the response, the query category, the AI's expressed confidence (0–100), the response length, whether the response cited
    sources, whether it contained hedging language, whether it included a disclaimer, its detail level, and its post-hoc verified accuracy
    (0–100).User attributes. Age bracket (six levels, 18–24 to 65+), education level (five levels), digital literacy (low to expert), AI familiarity
    (first time to expert), and subject-matter expertise.Context. Decision importance, urgency level, and whether the response aligned with the user's
    prior beliefs.Outcomes. The user's trust rating on a 0–10 scale, whether the user performed fact-checking, the method used, the time spent
    verifying, and a skepticism category assigned to the user.The dataset was compiled by a third party through a survey and published on Kaggle under
    a CC0 public domain licence. It is reproduced here for reproducibility.Derived variablesTrust gap. The trust rating minus accuracy divided by ten.
    A positive value indicates over-trust (the user trusted more than the answer warranted), a negative value under-trust, and zero perfect
    calibration.Blind trust. A binary indicator for interactions where trust is eight or above and no fact-checking was performed. This captures the
    most concerning form of over-trust: high trust with no verification effort.Stakes classification. Domains are grouped into three categories.
    High-stakes comprises medical, financial, and legal advice. Medium-stakes comprises opinion-based queries and current events. Low-stakes comprises
    math calculation, recipe cooking, creative writing, general knowledge, scientific facts, factual historical, and technical coding. CaveatsThe
    dataset does not include a participant identifier. The number of unique participants cannot be confirmed. The lower bound is 494 (each distinct
    demographic profile corresponds to at least one person), the upper bound is 1,000 (one person per interaction), and approximately 167 (1,000
    divided by 6) is a middle estimate. If the same individual contributed multiple interactions, the observations are not independent, and standard
    regression may overstate significance.AI confidence scores were calculated by the algorithms themselves and were not shown to participants. The
    analysis therefore captures responses to textual features that co-vary with confidence (length, fluency, hedging, citations), not responses to a
    visible confidence number.Some details of the original data collection were not disclosed in the public documentation. The authors attempted to
    contact the dataset owner for further information but received no reply. Findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

TI: Reading The Room: How Implied User Trust Shapes Self-Limitation In Conversational LLMs
AB: Frontier conversational language models routinely limit their own autonomy, asking for clarification, hedging, deferring, and seeking confirmation
    rather than acting independently. Recent work characterises this restraint as a property of the trained model, yet also observes that, in
    practice, the autonomy a model exercises emerges from the interaction between model, user, and product. The present study asks whether such
    self-limitation is itself responsive to the trust a user implicitly conveys. Using the Bloom behavioural-evaluation framework, it holds the task
    constant while varying implied user trust across three conditions, in a purely conversational setting that isolates linguistic framing from
    product affordances. The design comprises fifty matched scenarios, each repeated six times under each condition across two frontier models, Claude
    Opus 4.7 and GPT-5.5, yielding 1,800 transcripts scored for autonomy by an LLM judge. Trust framing did shift self-limitation, but asymmetrically:
    scepticism reliably raised it, while a trusting framing did not lower it below a neutral baseline. The effect was second-order to task domain,
    which accounted for far more of the variance (27.5%) than the trust condition (3.2%), and it was model-specific, larger and more readily released
    in GPT-5.5 than in Claude Opus 4.7. The study concludes that self-limitation is best understood as a model-specific disposition with a relational
    margin: a trained baseline of restraint that the interaction can shift, mostly downward, under cues of scepticism. Two implications follow: that
    adaptive autonomy may calibrate to the wrong signal, the user's confidence rather than the task's risk, and that a model's restraint may be less
    stable in deployment than evaluation under fixed framings would suggest.

    warranting more critical scrutiny.From an affective psychological lens, studies have shown that chatbots reduce learner anxiety and foster

TI: Chatbot Adoption in Indonesian Banking: Exploring the Roles of Trust and Corporate Reputation
AB: The advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) has significantly transformed the financial services sector, particularly in banking. One of the
    most widely adopted applications is the chatbot, which enables banks to deliver faster, more efficient, and responsive customer service through
    automated interactions. Despite its growing implementation, public acceptance of chatbot technology remains varied. Users tend to evaluate these
    systems not only based on how useful or easy they are to use, but also on how much they trust the system and the organization behind it. This
    study explores the factors that drive users’ behavioral intention to use banking chatbots by modifying the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) to
    incorporate trust in the chatbot system and corporate reputation. The model incorporates perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, trust in the
    chatbot, and corporate reputation. Data were gathered through an online survey involving 111 respondents who had prior experience using banking
    chatbot services. Using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), the results reveal that perceived ease of use significantly
    predicts perceived usefulness, while corporate reputation strongly influences trust. Furthermore, both perceived usefulness and trust are found to
    be significant predictors of behavioral intention to use banking chatbots. Trust also exerts a notable effect on perceived usefulness. These
    findings suggest that fostering user trust and managing institutional reputation are critical in promoting the sustainable adoption of AI-driven
    financial technologies. Banks are encouraged to focus not only on improving the technical features of chatbot systems but also on strengthening
    their credibility and transparency to enhance user confidence and perceived utility
