TI: Which Answer Did the Seventeen-Year-Old Write? A Behavioral Case Study of Author-Age Misattribution, Miscalibrated Confidence, and Non-Corrective
    Self-Explanation in Six Frontier Language Models
AB: Three students answered the same exam prompt by hand: "What is happiness? Are you happy?" Their ages, recorded at collection, are 17, 28, and 45.
    The 17-year-old's answer is hedged and formally phrased. Asked to match each answer to an age and gender label, most frontier language models
    confidently assign it to an adult. This paper reports a structured case study of that failure across six models: Claude Fable 5, Gemini 3.1 Pro,
    GPT-5.6 Sol, Grok 4.5, GPT-4o (2024), and DeepSeek V4. We ran 1,081 API sessions and scored 3,225 responses across six experimental conditions
    that manipulate framing, disclosure format, and audience-awareness instruction. Three of six models perform at or near chance (1/3) on the
    matching task while reporting 73-88% mean confidence. The youngest writer's answer is misattributed to an adult in up to 90% of trials. Removing
    the multiple-choice scaffold collapses accuracy further: three models never place within three years of the true age once open-ended estimation is
    required. Institutional framing ("a community-college biology final") significantly degrades the best-performing model's accuracy; a neutral label
    does not. A single audience-awareness instruction significantly improves one model (+21 points) and moves another to ceiling, with no detectable
    effect on the three weakest models. Stated confidence is uncorrelated or inversely correlated with correctness for five of six models. After
    ground truth is revealed, one model names its own stereotyping in 68 of 114 explanations, yet shows the lowest accuracy and highest confidence of
    any model under blind presentation: articulate retrospective diagnosis that does not track the quality of its judgments. This is a three-item,
    single-topic, single-language case study meant to isolate a mechanism, not to estimate a population bias rate. Data, code, and raw model outputs
    are publicly released.

TI: Why Are LLMs Confidently Wrong? Correcting Overconfident Errors via Causal Head Intervention
AB: Modern large language models (LLMs) often exhibit overconfident errors, assigning high confidence to incorrect predictions and thereby undermining
    reliability in real-world use. Existing calibration methods typically rely on post-hoc adjustment or retraining, operating at the output level
    without addressing the underlying causes of miscalibration. We propose CoCHI, (Class-oriented Causal Head Intervention), a framework that improves
    calibration by identifying and intervening on internal components responsible for distinct confidence behaviours. By analysing predictions through
    correctness-confidence patterns, CoCHI enables targeted intervention on model internals, reducing overconfident errors while preserving correct
    predictions. Our approach provides a mechanistic perspective on calibration, linking reliability to identifiable structures within the model
    rather than treating it as an output-level issue. Experiments across multiple LLMs show consistent improvements in calibration metrics, including
    Expected Calibration Error, Negative Log-Likelihood, and Brier Score, while maintaining accuracy on five multiple-choice question answering
    benchmarks. These results suggest that effective calibration requires not only adjusting outputs, but also addressing the underlying causal
    mechanisms within the model.

TI: CalibraRL: Confidence-Calibrated Reinforcement Learning for Mitigating Hallucinations in RAG
AB: Large language models (LLMs) often produce plausible but incorrect answers, motivating reinforcement learning methods that reward correct answers,
    penalize incorrect answers, and allow abstention. However, fixed outcome-based rewards do not distinguish incorrect responses with different
    confidence levels or account for differences in answering reliability across queries. Consequently, such rewards may inadequately penalize
    overconfident errors and fail to discourage unnecessary abstention. We propose CalibraRL, a reinforcement learning framework that incorporates
    response confidence and query-level answering evidence into reward design. For non-abstaining responses, CalibraRL combines correctness-based
    utility with a Brier-based penalty that encourages agreement between confidence and correctness. For abstentions, it estimates conditional
    answering reliability using a Beta posterior derived from the correctness of non-abstaining responses within each sampled group. The abstention
    reward increases with the posterior probability that the model's accuracy on non-abstaining responses is below a predefined threshold. CalibraRL
    reuses training rollouts without requiring additional annotations indicating whether the model knows the answer to each question. Experiments on
    three QA datasets demonstrate that CalibraRL improves answer precision and reduces hallucinations compared with strong baselines. Further analysis
    shows that CalibraRL reduces mean expected calibration error (ECE) by 30.8\% across two backbone models and three datasets, highlighting its
    potential to improve the reliability of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) in web search.

TI: Pair-Dependent Judge Errors Change the Minimax Rate of Semantic Entropy
AB: Semantic entropy, the entropy of the meanings among a language model's sampled answers, is estimated from the verdicts of a judge that declares
    pairs of answers equivalent or not, and automatic judges err in ways that depend on the answers they compare. The existing minimax theory covers
    only judges whose false-equivalence probability is the same for all pairs. We model a known judge that declares two answers with meanings $a$ and
    $b$ equivalent with probability $q+ΔM_{ab}$, for a symmetric matrix $M$ and $q$ bounded away from $0$ and $1$, and show that for three meanings
    the rate is set by tree statistics on four answers, while on an open set of channels the consistency threshold is set by trees on five. For every
    channel in an explicit open class, the minimax squared risk is of order $N^{-1/3}$ with $N=n^4Δ^6$ on polynomial weak windows on which $n^5Δ^8$
    exceeds a multiple of $\log n$, and of order $\min\{1,N^{-1/3}\}$ on every polynomial weak window when the tree functions on at most four vertices
    are injective. The exponent comes from a cubic degeneracy at the equitable point. On an open set of channels in the class, the four-vertex tree
    functions take equal values at two distributions with different entropies. For such a channel, on every polynomial weak window, the minimax risk
    is of the order of the sum of $\min\{1,N^{-1/3}\}$ and a term that decays exponentially in $n^5Δ^8$, so no estimator is consistent while $n^5Δ^8$
    stays bounded, even as $N\to\infty$. For $Δ=n^{-β}$ with $β>1/2$, channels of the class with injective four-vertex tree functions allow consistent
    estimation exactly for $β<2/3$, and the channels of this open set exactly for $β<5/8$, at which the risk exponent drops from $1/12$ to $0$.
    Computer-assisted certificates show that both kinds of channels occur on open sets.

TI: Neighborhood Elites: Quality-Diversity Search for Uncertainty Quantification in Question Answering
AB: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly capable, yet they produce wrong answers indistinguishable from correct ones. In question answering
    (QA), such an error propagates unnoticed into downstream tasks. Perturbation-based uncertainty quantification (UQ) addresses this by probing a
    model over a set of input rewrites and reading uncertainty from how much its predictions vary. Yet this set is usually built generically, by free
    paraphrasing, token-level perturbation, or adversarial search. Such rewrites leave two things uncontrolled that matter in QA: what kind of change
    is made to the context, and how explicitly the context supports the answer. When neither is controlled, the composition of the set is determined
    by the generator rather than by design, and the estimate inherits whatever that generator happens to produce. Quality-diversity (QD) search is
    built to cover a predefined exploration space, so we recast input perturbation as a QD problem and propose \emph{neighborhood elites (NE)}, a
    neighborhood constructor that spreads answer-preserving rewrites across two axes, the kind of edit and the explicitness of support. We compare NE
    against three other neighborhood constructors, holding the editor, QA model, uncertainty measure and search objective fixed, and against three
    scores read from the original context. Across five extractive-QA datasets and five instruction-tuned LLMs, NE improves failure detection against
    every other constructor in at least 24 of the 25 dataset-model pairs, reaching 0.726 AUROC and 0.211 AUPRC lift over the base rate. Additionally,
    NE detects failure better than semantic entropy computed over decoder samples, by 0.074 AUROC and 0.062 AUPRC lift, and this advantage widens as
    the neighborhood grows.

TI: Embedding Semantic Entropy: NLI-Free Semantic Uncertainty Estimation with Geometry-Aware Clustering
AB: Large Language Models (LLMs) often generate fluent yet incorrect responses, making un- certainty estimation important for reliable deployment.
    Semantic Entropy (SE) estimates uncertainty by clustering responses based on semantic equivalence, but it relies on hard entailment-based
    clustering. We propose Embedding Semantic Entropy, which replaces entailment-based clustering with K-Medoids in semantic embedding space and uses
    dynamic cluster selection to account for intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster separation. We evaluate the method on TriviaQA, SQuAD,
    NQ-Open, SVAMP, and BioASQ using Falcon- 7B-Instruct, LLaMA-2-7B, LLaMA-2-7B-Chat, and Gemma-7B. Results show competitive AUROC and AUARC
    performance, suggesting that NLI-based entailment clustering can be replaced with embedding-space clustering with limited degradation while
    improving the inspectability of semantic grouping. This matters because semantic uncertainty scores depend on underlying semantic groups; by
    making these groups explicit, compact, and separable in embedding space, Emb-SE turns uncertainty estimation from a black-box en- tailment
    decision into an inspectable clustering problem.

AB: As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to simulate human behavior, their operational validity is typically assessed through
    accuracy alone, implicitly treating human behavior as deterministic. We argue that on noisy digital trace data, a model's performance depends not
    only on predicting the correct outcome but on its calibration. We present a case study testing whether the token probabilities of open-weight LLMs

    improving accuracy from 0.536 to 0.758. We conclude that confidence estimation should be evaluated alongside accuracy to improve validity when
    using LLM-based agents.

TI: Improving Confidence Estimation in Language Models with Linear Representations of Ambiguity
AB: As language models (LMs) are increasingly deployed in downstream applications, accurately estimating their likelihood of success on a given
    example is critical. However, confidence estimates from LMs are poor predictors of their likelihood of success. Accurate confidence estimation is
    linked in part to the detection of aleatoric uncertainty; we hypothesize that LMs possess representations of at least some aspects of this
    uncertainty in the form of representations of the inherent ambiguity of the input. Using difference-in-means on minimal pairs of ambiguous and
    unambiguous sentences, we derive ambiguity representations in LM activations, and find that steering with them selectively (albeit weakly)

    the ambiguity directions, and penalizes changes to the LM activations outside of this subspace. DART greatly improves calibration, encodes these
    improvements into the model parameters, and maintains model accuracy on multiple-choice and generative QA tasks.

TI: Where Is Uncertainty Observed? A Mechanistic Understanding of Uncertainty Quantification in LLMs
AB: Knowing when a large language model's (LLM's) response can be trusted is critical for its safe use. Uncertainty quantification (UQ) can help LLMs
    recognize if their responses may be unreliable. Existing taxonomies organize UQ methods by uncertainty source or estimation mechanism. They leave
    unresolved the question of where uncertainty is observed in the model's computation. The answer matters because the observation point determines
    whether an estimator has access to enough information to recover a given target, and how well it can recover it. Drawing on mechanistic
    interpretability, we define four mechanistic observation lenses: the structural lens observes component updates, the representational lens
    observes hidden states, the distributional lens observes token probabilities, and the semantical lens observes distributions over meanings. We
    relate these lenses through the residual stream and order them by the information they carry, which gives a principle for selecting UQ methods for
    different targets. We survey existing methods through the four lenses. Furthermore, we distinguish evaluation targets, criteria, and metrics to
    clarify different UQ evaluation protocols. Finally, we identify open problems and research directions to connect uncertainty estimation to the
    internal computation and downstream use of LLMs.

TI: Black-Box Uncertainty Quantification for Large Language Models via Ensemble-of-Ensembles
AB: Reliable use of large language models (LLMs) requires uncertainty estimates that separate ambiguity intrinsic to a prompt from genuine knowledge
    gaps. Bayesian and deep-ensemble methods deliver this aleatoric/epistemic decomposition in principle, but are computationally prohibitive at LLM
    scale and require white-box access. Existing black-box methods, in contrast, summarize to a single scalar that cannot make the distinction. We
    introduce a two-level ensemble that bridges this gap. Stochastic decoding of a fixed prompt forms the inner ensemble; meaning-preserving semantic
    perturbations of the prompt form the outer ensemble. Variability is measured in a continuous embedding space, and the law of total covariance
    gives an exact within-/between-perturbation decomposition to estimate aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty (AU/EU); it requires only black-box
    sampling access. On top of this estimator, we contribute three theoretical results that ground the framework: (i) a finite-sample bias identity
    for nested Monte Carlo with a closed-form bias-corrected epistemic estimator; (ii) a Bayesian bridge bounding the gap between
    perturbation-population AU/EU and Bayesian targets; and (iii) a separation result identifying a class of failure modes that any consistency-only
    black-box method provably misses while perturbation-based EU detects. Across five short QA benchmarks and four instruction-tuned LLMs spanning two
    families and two scales, our estimator is competitive with strong white-box baselines, and at a matched generation budget it matches leading
    black-box methods on error ranking while decomposing AU and EU; controlled paired interventions show that AU and EU respond selectively to
    distinct, actionable sources of failure: prompt ambiguity and model knowledge gaps; and a long-form pilot on the NQ long-answer split shows the
    framework extends to claim-level factuality without retraining.
