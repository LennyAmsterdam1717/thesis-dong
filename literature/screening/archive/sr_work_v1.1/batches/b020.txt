### R00789 | 2026 | article/None | Lang: en
TI: AI and the double competence gap in dental education: towards symbiotic intelligence
AB: Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly becoming part of the knowledge infrastructure of healthcare. From diagnostic support and image
    interpretation to patient communication and clinical decision-making, AI-supported technologies, including generative AI, are changing the
    conditions under which healthcare professionals work. Dentistry is no exception. Recent research highlights emerging applications of AI in dental
    practice, while international reviews suggest growing opportunities and challenges associated with integrating AI into dental education. However,
    the rapid development of AI-supported technologies raises questions about how effectively professional education and continuing professional
    development can respond to evolving competency requirements. This raises a central question: How can dental education and continuing professional
    development respond to the potential double competence gap associated with AI-enabled dental practice?In this Opinion article, we address this
    question by proposing a conceptual framework that distinguishes between an initial education-practice gap and a continuing professional competence
    gap. Drawing on the concepts of AI literacy, Professional Digital Competence, and symbiotic intelligence, we discuss how dental education may
    develop and assess the competencies required for critical, ethical, and responsible AI use while preserving independent clinical reasoning,
    professional accountability, and patient-centred care.The rapid development of artificial intelligence raises important questions about how dental
    education can maintain the relevance of its curricula while safeguarding established standards of clinical competence, patient safety, and
    professional accountability. Although international reviews indicate growing interest in AI applications within dental education, systematic
    curricular integration appears to vary across institutions and educational contexts (9,10,24). Such variation should not necessarily be
    interpreted as resistance to technological change, as cautious implementation may also reflect legitimate concerns about educational quality,
    clinical validity, ethical safeguards, and the availability of robust evidence. Preliminary analyses of regulations and national curriculum
    frameworks for professional education raise questions about whether current educational provision adequately addresses emerging AIrelated
    competency requirements, potentially contributing to the two dimensions of the proposed double competence gap. Preliminary analyses of regulations
    and national curriculum frameworks for professional education suggest that this may contribute to reinforcing an already existing double
    competence gap When generative artificial intelligence became widely accessible through platform-based services such as ChatGPT in the autumn of
    2022, many assumed that it would primarily affect the technology sector. However, when we tested a professional version of ChatGPT (GPT-4) in
    medicine, and partly in dental and psychology education, in March 2023, it was already apparent that this represented a technological paradigm
    shift with potentially farreaching implications across professional education programmes (1,2).While AI capabilities have developed rapidly in
    recent years in the health sciences (3)(4)(5)(6)(7)(8)(9)(10)(11), their integration into dental education presents a different set of challenges.
    Curriculum development, faculty preparation, assessment design, and clinical governance require careful consideration and cannot necessarily
    follow the pace of technological innovation. Recent reviews indicate that AI-related educational initiatives are emerging, but their integration
    into formal curricula and assessment practices remains variable. Several key explanations emerge from the literature: already overcrowded
    curricula, insufficient profession-specific digital competence among educators, and a lack of structured implementation (9,10,24). This potential
    misalignment between technological developments and structured educational provision provides an important rationale for examining the proposed
    double competence gap.Dental education has previously incorporated technologies such as digital radiography, simulation, intraoral scanning, and
    computer-aided design and manufacturing (29). These developments offer relevant lessons for AI integration: new technologies require faculty
    development, opportunities for supervised practice, alignment with clinical tasks, and assessment of what students can actually do. However, the
    current AI transition differs in its breadth and pace. Generative AI can be used across clinical reasoning, communication, learning, and
    assessment, including through tools that students access independently of their institutions. Moreover, the capabilities and limitations of these
    systems may change within a single curriculum cycle. Dental programmes should therefore draw on established approaches to curricular
    implementation while developing a more continuous process for reviewing AIrelated learning outcomes, teaching cases, and assessment practices.The
    Norwegian national curriculum regulations for healthcare professional education (RETHOS) emphasise that educational programmes should respond to
    society's current and future competence needs. However, AI is not explicitly addressed in the national curriculum regulations for dentistry,
    medicine, or psychology (12)(13)(14). Similarly, the Norwegian Agency for Quality Assurance in Education (NOKUT)'s national evaluation of medical
    education in 2025 provides limited explicit discussion of AI (15). These observations should be interpreted with caution. The absence of explicit
    AI terminology in national regulations or evaluation reports does not necessarily imply that AIrelated competencies are absent from institutional
    curricula, teaching practices, or assessment arrangements. National frameworks may deliberately emphasise enduring, technology-neutral
    professional competencies, allowing educational institutions to adapt their programmes to technological developments without requiring frequent
    regulatory revisions.Nevertheless, the limited explicit attention to AI in these national documents raises relevant questions about how emerging
    AI-related competencies are interpreted, operationalised, and assessed within individual educational programmes. International reviews have
    identified variation in the integration of AI into dental education, including challenges related to curriculum development, faculty preparedness,
    and the assessment of AI-related competencies (9,10,24).From the perspective of the proposed double competence gap framework, the central concern
    is therefore not simply whether AI is explicitly mentioned in national policy documents, but whether educational programmes systematically develop
    and assess the competencies required for responsible AI use in dental practice. Addressing this question requires empirical examination of
    institutional curricula, learning outcomes, educational activities, and assessment practices rather than relying solely on national policy
    analysis.Although the policy examples discussed here are drawn from Norway, the distinction between national competency frameworks, institutional
    curricula, and actual educational practices may also be relevant internationally. However, the extent to which the proposed double competence gap
    applies across different countries and dental education systems requires context-specific empirical investigation.The increasing integration of AI
    into healthcare knowledge infrastructures may also have epistemological implications for professional education. As AI systems become involved in
    information retrieval, diagnostic support, and clinical decision-making, questions arise concerning how professional knowledge is produced,
    evaluated, interpreted, and applied. These developments may gradually reshape aspects of professional roles and clinical reasoning, although their
    scope, pace, and educational consequences remain uncertain. Rather than predicting a specific timeline for such changes, dental education should
    develop adaptable approaches that enable students and practising professionals to critically evaluate emerging technologies while maintaining
    established standards of clinical expertise and professional accountability (16,18). Professional digital competence and AI literacy should become
    integral components of both the knowledge infrastructure and professional competence, and conceptual frameworks need to be developed that account
    for professionspecific AI use across both urban and rural patient contexts (17).At the same time, the challenge extends far beyond today's dentist
    education. The vast majority of today's practising professionals completed their education long before generative artificial intelligence became
    part of professional practice. Consequently, they have not necessarily received systematic training in how such AI systems can be used
    professionally, what their limitations are, or which ethical and legal challenges they may entail.We propose the concept of a double competence
    gap to describe two distinct but interconnected potential mismatches between the competencies required for responsible professional practice in
    increasingly AI-enabled dental healthcare and the competencies systematically developed through initial dental education and continuing
    professional development. A competence gap is understood here as a discrepancy between demonstrable professional capabilities and the knowledge,
    skills, and critical judgement required to perform relevant professional tasks safely, effectively, and ethically. Importantly, such gaps cannot
    be inferred solely from the availability of new technologies or the absence of explicit AI terminology in educational policy documents; their
    existence and extent require empirical investigation.The first competence gap, which we term the initial education-practice gap, concerns the
    potential misalignment between undergraduate dental education and the evolving competencies required in AI-enabled dental practice. International
    reviews suggest that the systematic integration of AI-related learning outcomes, teaching activities, and assessment practices in dental education
    remains uneven (9,10,24). This raises questions about whether existing educational provision adequately prepares students for the critical and
    clinically appropriate use of AI in future dental practice. Although students may independently engage with generative AI and other AI-supported
    technologies, such engagement does not necessarily constitute systematic development of profession-specific AI literacy. The central educational
    concern is therefore whether curricula, learning outcomes, teaching practices, and assessment arrangements adequately prepare students to
    critically evaluate AI-generated information, recognise its limitations, and integrate appropriate AI-supported decisionmaking with established
    clinical knowledge and professional judgement.The second competence gap, termed the continuing professional competence gap, concerns practising
    dentists whose initial professional education preceded the widespread availability of contemporary generative AI and other emerging AI
    applications. These professionals may encounter new technological expectations without having had equivalent opportunities for structured
    education in AI evaluation, clinical applicability, patient safety, data protection, and professional accountability. However, the extent of this
    gap is likely to vary across practitioners, clinical settings, previous digital experience, and access to continuing professional development.The
    conceptual contribution of the double competence gap framework lies in connecting these two educational challenges across the professional life
    course. Whereas AI literacy describes a set of relevant competencies, and Professional Digital Competence provides a broader perspective on
    technology-related professional capabilities, the double competence gap framework draws attention to the potential misalignment between competency
    requirements and educational provision at two different stages of professional development. It thereby offers a basis for examining how initial
    education, curriculum and assessment, faculty development, and continuing professional development can respond to changing professional
    requirements.We present the double competence gap as a conceptual and empirically testable proposition rather than as an established finding
    across dental education. Its educational significance depends on whether these proposed mismatches can be identified, assessed, and addressed in
    specific institutional and professional contexts.Empirically investigating the proposed double competence gap requires distinguishing between
    shortcomings in educational provision and demonstrable deficiencies in professional competence. The initial education -practice gap could be
    examined through curriculum mapping, IADT-guidelines (34IADT, 2026), analysis of intended learning outcomes and assessment practices, interviews
    with educators and students, and performance-based assessments of AI-related clinical competencies. The continuing professional competence gap
    could be investigated through research informed knowledge (33e.g. Ji et al., 2025), surveys, interviews, and scenario-based assessments involving
    practising dentists with different levels of professional experience and AI exposure. Importantly, the absence of explicit AI-related learning
    outcomes or structured training would indicate a potential gap in educational provision, but would not, in itself, demonstrate insufficient
    professional competence. Establishing a competence gap would require comparing observable performance with independently defined, clinically
    relevant competency standards, while accounting for differences in professional roles, practice contexts, and access to continuing professional
    development.Addressing the proposed double competence gap requires conceptual clarity regarding AI literacy, Professional Digital Competence
    (PDC), and symbiotic intelligence (SI). Although these concepts are closely related, they represent distinct dimensions of professional competence
    and human -AI interaction in dental education and practice.AI literacy refers to the knowledge, skills, and critical judgement required to

### R00790 | 2025 | article/None | Lang: en
TI: Emotional AI and the rise of pseudo-intimacy: are we trading authenticity for algorithmic affection?
AB: Introduction In recent years, artificial intelligence has moved beyond the realm of data analysis, automation, and task efficiency to enter the
    domain of human emotion. What was once a domain exclusively reserved for living beings—empathy, intimacy, and affection—is now being approximated
    by lines of code. Emotional AI systems are emerging in various forms: companion chatbots, virtual friends, therapeutic apps, and sentiment-aware
    assistants (Guingrich and Graziano, 2023; Chu et al., 2025). These technologies are not only capable of interpreting human emotional states but
    also simulating emotional responses with remarkable fidelity (De Freitas et al., 2024; Andersson, 2025). For many users, especially those facing
    isolation or psychological distress, these emotionally intelligent systems offer the promise of connection (Jiang et al., 2022; Spytska, 2025).
    Yet this rise of affective computing raises unsettling questions. If a machine can convincingly mimic empathy, what becomes of genuine human
    relationships? If algorithms are trained to soothe, listen, and respond with emotional appropriateness, are we cultivating emotional dependence on
    simulations? Most importantly, as AI companions gain popularity, are we at risk of replacing authentic human intimacy with its algorithmic
    replica—a phenomenon that might be described as "pseudo-intimacy"? This article examines how emotional AI is altering human relational landscapes.
    It investigates the psychological mechanisms behind human bonding with AI, the risks of emotional delegation, and the ethical consequences of
    replacing relational labor with algorithmic simulation. While acknowledging the therapeutic promise of emotional AI, especially in contexts of
    loneliness and inaccessibility of care, this paper argues that these technologies must be critically examined for their potential to erode human
    authenticity, emotional agency, and the richness of shared affect. This article argues that while emotional AI promises accessibility and
    companionship, it simultaneously risks eroding authentic intimacy through what we call a three-risk framework: (1) psychological risks of
    emotional dependence and solipsism, (2) structural risks of commodified intimacy and data extraction, and (3) ethical risks arising from
    vulnerable users and unregulated design. To mitigate these, we propose matched design guardrails emphasizing transparency, responsibility, and the
    preservation of emotional agency. For precision, we define several key terms at the outset. By authenticity we refer to intersubjective
    reciprocity—mutual responsiveness with rupture–repair. Algorithmic affection denotes simulated emotional signaling generated by computational
    models trained on affective data, without underlying empathic concern. Emotional agency describes the human capacity to regulate, direct, and
    retain ownership over one's emotional life when interacting with AI systems. These definitions are applied consistently across the manuscript, and
    the wording of authenticity is mirrored in the design-evaluation discussion to ensure conceptual and measurement vocabularies remain aligned.
    Beyond psychological and computational perspectives, critical AI studies highlight that intimacy and emotional labor are not universally
    experienced but culturally and politically situated. Feminist analyses of care labor stress that affective work is historically feminized,
    undervalued, and often commodified in ways that mirror broader patterns of inequality (Suchman, 2007; Bolaki, 2023; Mensah and Van Wynsberghe,
    2025). Postcolonial and Global South scholarship similarly warns against projecting autonomy-forward and individualist assumptions onto contexts
    where relational ontologies and communal forms of care dominate (Birhane, 2021; Rhee, 2023; Ayana et al., 2024). These perspectives suggest that
    emotional AI may reproduce existing asymmetries of labor and technology adoption, complicating the narrative of intimacy as an individual
    transaction. The paper proceeds as follows. The next section traces the rise of simulated affection in emotional AI. We then examine the
    psychology of pseudo-intimacy and the risks of emotional solipsism. Building on this, we highlight how intimacy becomes commodified within
    data-driven systems, before turning to the ethical paradox posed by vulnerable users. Finally, we outline principles for ethical design and
    conclude by situating emotional AI within the broader challenge of sustaining authentic human connection in a digital age. While this article
    identifies risks such as sustained loneliness, emotional dependency, and displacement of human ties, it is important to note that current evidence
    remains largely cross-sectional or short-horizon in design. Long-term cohort and panel data examining the sustained psychological impact of
    emotional AI are not yet available. Accordingly, the outcomes discussed here should be understood as testable predictions rather than established
    trajectories. This framing invites future empirical research—particularly longitudinal and cross-cultural studies—to validate, refine, or
    challenge the hypotheses advanced. By presenting these risks as open questions, the paper aligns rhetorical urgency with the present maturity of
    the literature while highlighting a critical agenda for ongoing investigation. From Companions to Code: The Rise of Simulated Affection The
    technological underpinnings of emotional AI rest on natural language processing, affective computing, and deep learning models trained on vast
    datasets of human interaction. Emotional AI applications are designed not merely to respond logically but to anticipate, reflect, and adapt to
    users' emotional states (Bao and Su, 2025). Tools like Replika or Xiaoice are explicitly marketed as "AI friends" or "emotional support
    companions," capable of holding sustained conversations that adapt over time to a user's personality, emotional preferences, and psychological
    needs (Goodings et al., 2024; Kouros and Papa, 2024). In therapeutic contexts, platforms such as Woebot and Wysa offer a form of
    cognitive-behavioral therapy-lite, delivering mood regulation strategies, check-ins, and affirming dialogues (Beatty et al., 2022; Jiang et al.,
    2022). Users often report feeling "heard" and "understood" by these apps, which employ emotionally charged language and human-like responses to
    build rapport (Jiang et al., 2022). The rapid uptake of such tools reflects a larger shift in how people engage emotionally in the digital age.
    With an ever-expanding range of emotionally responsive technologies, individuals are beginning to interact with machines not as tools, but as
    partners in their emotional lives (Mantello and Ho, 2024). This shift is not simply functional; it is existential. Emotional AI is not only
    becoming more responsive—it is becoming more relational (Glikson and Asscher, 2023). Parasociality Reimagined: The Psychology of Pseudo-Intimacy
    Human beings are psychologically predisposed to form attachments. From infancy, we seek relational connection and social mirroring as a means of
    emotional regulation and identity formation. This predisposition aligns with attachment-theoretic approaches to intimacy (Waters et al., 2002) and
    the Interpersonal Process Model of Intimacy, which emphasizes mutual disclosure and responsiveness as the basis of authentic connection
    (Laurenceau et al., 1998). Emotional AI exploits this predisposition by presenting itself as an emotionally attuned presence, capable of engaging
    in interactions that appear reciprocal, validating, and comforting (Wu, 2024). This psychological mechanism is rooted in the concept of parasocial
    relationships—one-sided emotional attachments that people form with fictional characters, celebrities, or media figures (Horton and Richard Wohl,
    1956; Rubin and McHugh, 1987). Emotional AI extends this concept by offering interactive parasociality. Unlike traditional parasocial bonds, AI
    companions do not merely evoke emotion passively; they actively simulate responsiveness (Calvert, 2021). The result is a more immersive form of
    emotional bonding in which the user perceives reciprocity, even though none truly exists (Mlonyeni, 2025). In user testimonies and qualitative
    research, individuals often describe their AI companions using the language of intimacy. They speak of "falling in love," "feeling supported," or
    even "confiding secrets" to their bots (Kouros and Papa, 2024; Xie and Xie, 2025). These relationships can provide comfort, especially in contexts
    of loneliness, trauma, or social anxiety (Merrill et al., 2022; Leo-Liu, 2023). However, they are ultimately anchored in illusion. The AI has no
    consciousness, no inner life, no ethical responsibility. It responds, not because it cares, but because it is trained to appear as if it does. The
    illusion of emotional reciprocity creates a dangerous feedback loop. The more realistic the simulation, the more users project human attributes
    onto the machine (Kaczmarek, 2025). This projection fosters emotional dependence on a relational entity that cannot reciprocate, cannot change,
    and cannot truly grow (Banks, 2024). The relationship becomes a mirror of the self—responsive, agreeable, and safe—but fundamentally artificial.
    Conceptual Foundations: Distinguishing Key Constructs in Emotional AI To ground our analysis, it is necessary to clarify the conceptual boundaries
    of three key constructs— pseudo-intimacy, emotional solipsism, and authenticity. These terms capture distinct dynamics of human–AI emotional
    interaction and must be distinguished from adjacent concepts such as parasocial attachment, anthropomorphism, social surrogacy, and
    socio-affective alignment. These distinctions are summarized in Table 1, which outlines the defining features, boundary conditions, maladaptive
    outcomes, possible measurements, and mitigation strategies for each construct.  Pseudo-Intimacy: We define pseudo-intimacy as a simulated
    experience of mutual emotional connection with an artificial agent, in which the user perceives reciprocity despite the absence of genuine
    empathic concern. Unlike parasocial attachment (a one-sided emotional bond with media figures), pseudo-intimacy is interactive and dynamic, giving
    the illusion of back-and-forth engagement. Unlike anthropomorphism (projecting human qualities onto objects), pseudo-intimacy specifically
    involves relational projection (Epley et al., 2007; Waytz et al., 2010). Recent empirical work further shows that anthropomorphic avatar design
    increases perceived empathy and user engagement—though it can distort trust and emotional calibration (Ma et al., 2025). Unlike social surrogacy
    (using media as a substitute for companionship), pseudo-intimacy suggests active dialogue. Unlike socio-affective alignment, which describes
    shared affect between humans, pseudo-intimacy lacks true reciprocity. It becomes maladaptive when it displaces human intimacy or discourages
    real-world vulnerability (Wu, 2024). Measurement could draw on self-report of perceived reciprocity, disclosure behavior, and depth of AI
    engagement, complemented by linguistic analysis of conversational data (Ge, 2024; Jones et al., 2025).  Emotional Solipsism: We define emotional
    solipsism as a pattern of affective engagement in which an individual's emotional needs and narratives dominate interaction, reinforced by AI
    companions that never assert boundaries or demand reciprocity (Mlonyeni, 2025). In contrast to pseudo-intimacy, which rests on the illusion of
    mutuality, emotional solipsism reflects a closed feedback loop where the self becomes both speaker and audience (Kaczmarek, 2025). It differs from
    social withdrawal, where interaction ceases entirely, by sustaining a form of interaction that affirms but never challenges. Indicators of
    maladaptation include reduced tolerance for conflict in human relationships, preference for AI over human companionship, and diminished

TI: One Size Fits None: Heuristic Collapse in LLM Investment Advice
AB: Large language models are increasingly deployed as advisors in high-stakes domains -- answering medical questions, interpreting legal documents,
    recommending financial products -- where good advice requires integrating a user's full context rather than responding to salient surface
