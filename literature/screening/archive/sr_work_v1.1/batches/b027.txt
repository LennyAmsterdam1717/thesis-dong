TI: Anthropomorphism and risk perception: The role of trust and domain knowledge in decision-support AI
AB: Anthropomorphic design is increasingly used to make conversational AI systems more approachable and socially engaging, yet its effects on users’
    risk judgments remain unclear. Drawing on theories of anthropomorphism and cognitive–affective trust, we develop a moderated mediation model
    explaining how perceived anthropomorphism relates to risk perception in AI decision support. We conducted a large-scale between-subjects online
    experiment (N = 1,256) using a simulated financial advisor chatbot with a 2 2 2 anthropomorphic design manipulation varying visual cues (human vs.
    machine avatar), identity cues (human name vs. bot identifier), and communication style (human-like vs. machine-like communication). Results show
    that perceived anthropomorphism was positively associated with both cognitive trust and affective trust, but these trust dimensions had different
    relationships with perceived risk. Cognitive trust consistently predicted lower risk perception, whereas affective trust showed a weaker positive
    association with risk perception that was not robust across model specifications. Self-reported domain knowledge moderated these relationships:
    higher domain knowledge attenuated the association between perceived anthropomorphism and both trust dimensions, while participants with lower
    domain knowledge showed a stronger negative indirect association between anthropomorphism and risk perception through cognitive trust. By treating
    the perceived risk of following AI advice as a focal outcome, this study extends anthropomorphism research beyond trust formation and
    distinguishes how cognitive and affective trust relate to users’ risk judgments. The findings highlight the importance of calibrating, rather than
    simply maximizing, anthropomorphic cues in high-stakes AI decision-support systems.

TI: Auditing Memory Attribution in a Trading-Agent Replay
AB: This paper presents a paired attribution audit of teacher-forced trading-agent replays using a frozen SPY historical replay. The study examines
    whether observed action changes can be attributed to retrieved return content rather than to surrounding risk wording, recommendations, or
    authority framing. Across three experiments, surrounding guidance reproduced the tested Llama action shifts, while the tested negative-only versus
    balanced return-composition substitutions produced no observed action switches. These results are configuration-specific and do not establish that
    retrieved facts were ignored. The task lacked an objective evidence-dependent decision rule, and the historical records do not provide a complete
    raw-attempt stream. The paper therefore treats the findings as a bounded attribution audit and identifies the evidence-use positive control
    required for stronger conclusions. No live trading, investment recommendation, or general claim about language-model memory is made.

    researchers seeking to extend the service-quality literature into the fintech domain.
