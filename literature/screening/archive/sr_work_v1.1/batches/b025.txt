AB: Artificial intelligence (AI) features in FinTech-enabled digital banking support financial decisions but also introduce technological risks, such
    as black-box reasoning and erroneous recommendations. Studies often treat customer-facing AI as a single construct, leaving it unclear whether
    customers respond to explanation consistently across features. Drawing on the Stimulus–Organism–Response framework, this study models perceived AI
    explainability as a stimulus related to continuance intention through algorithmic trust (a cognitive pathway) and AI anxiety (an affective

    anxiety is more strongly associated with lower continuance among robo-advisor users, and explainability with lower anxiety among chatbot users,
    the latter not being robust to multiple-comparison adjustment. The findings inform customer-side research on AI in a technological risk context
    and motivate, but do not test, a common explanation standard complemented by human oversight for financial recommendation features.

AB: Abstract Large language models (LLMs) offer opportunities for scalable financial guidance, but hallucinations, numerical errors, insufficient
    grounding, and unstable decisions limit their use in high-stakes financial settings. This study develops and evaluates a multiagent AI framework
    for retirement-oriented financial guidance using synthetic profiles modelled on displaced Ukrainians in Poland. The framework combines
    retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), structured profile generation, deterministic budget calculations, and an architecturally separate Evaluator
    Agent. The primary computational evaluation comprised 500 synthetic advisory cases. Additional experiments included a paired no-RAG ablation on 50
    fixed profiles and a repeated-run stability analysis of 100 fixed profiles replayed five times. Under the predefined automated routing criteria,
    327 cases were accepted, 170 required review, and 3 were flagged. After the deterministic budget overwrite, a separate deterministic arithmetic
    check found zero profile-to-answer mismatches. In the paired ablation, the mean final composite score was 8.40 with RAG and 7.08 without RAG. The
    difference was driven primarily by larger deterministic penalty deductions and grounding-gate failures without RAG, while the mean of the five LLM
    rubric dimensions did not improve with RAG. In repeated runs, only 14% of profiles retained identical routing across all five replays. The
    deterministic arithmetic pathway remained invariant, while generation, retrieval, model-based evaluation, and threshold-based routing varied
    across repeated execution. The framework is therefore interpreted as a controlled computational testbed and quality-assurance architecture rather
    than a validated autonomous financial advisor.

TI: Impact of Artificial Intelligence on Investment Decision-Making among Retail Investors
AB: Retail investors increasingly use artificial intelligence (AI) tools such as robo-advisors, screeners and chatbots, but it is unclear whether this
    improves decisions or amplifies bias. This study aims to evaluate the impact of AI-enabled investment tools and behavioral biases on the
    investment decision quality of Indian retail investors, with financial literacy examined as a moderating factor. A quantitative survey design was
    used. Data came from a structured questionnaire of twenty five-point Likert statements completed by 255 retail investors who use AI-driven
    platforms, and were analysed with descriptive statistics, Cronbach’s alpha, Pearson correlation and t-tests in SPSS. Respondents reported
    moderately high AI usage (mean 3.603), high financial literacy (3.835) and moderate behavioral bias (3.247). AI usage correlated positively with
    investment outcomes (r = 0.168, p = 0.007) and with bias scores (r = 0.211, p < 0.001), but not with financial literacy (r = 0.085, p = 0.176).
    Overconfidence, information overload, herding and the disposition effect were significant among AI users; overtrading was not (p = 0.068). The
    findings suggest that AI is a modest decision-support tool that coexists with persistent behavioral bias rather than removing it. For practice,
    platform developers should add explainability and bias-mitigating prompts, and regulators should prioritize disclosure and investor education.
    Future research should use longitudinal designs and linked brokerage records to test causality. In conclusion, AI tools help retail investors only
    modestly, so they work best when combined with financial knowledge and disciplined judgement.
