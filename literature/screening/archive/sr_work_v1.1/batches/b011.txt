AB: Large reasoning models (LRMs) have achieved strong performance enhancement through scaling test time computation, but due to the inherent
    limitations of the underlying language models, they still have shortcomings in tasks that require precise computation and extensive knowledge

    empirical studies on uncertainty communication have treated uncertainty as a single numerical score or simple natural language expression. This
    simplification fails to capture a key property of LLM outputs: a single response often comprises multiple claims and reasoning steps, each with
    distinct levels of uncertainty. To address this gap, this study investigates uncertainty granularity (i.e., the extent to which uncertainty is
    expressed at different levels within an LLM response) and examines its impact on LLM-assisted decision-making. We conducted a large-scale,
    between-subjects study (N=192) in which participants answered medical questions using LLMs that displayed uncertainty at three different
    granularities: output-level (entire response), relation-level (individual reasoning steps), and token-level (specific words). Our findings reveal
    distinct behavioral effects as a function of uncertainty granularity. Token-level uncertainty increased users' agreement with the AI, whereas
    output- and relation-level uncertainty did not increase agreement but instead reduced users' confidence in their own answers. Notably,
    relation-level uncertainty also reduced external verification (i.e., internet searches, checking provided URLs), steering users away from
    independent fact-checking and toward reliance on the LLM and its accompanying uncertainty cues. Our findings demonstrate that uncertainty
    granularity significantly shapes how users interact with and verify LLM outputs, providing concrete design guidance for building responsible LLM
    applications that encourage appropriate skepticism and verification behaviors.

TI: Large language models in undergraduate medical education - student use, perceptions, and outcomes: a systematic review (Preprint)
AB: BACKGROUND Large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, are increasingly being used in medical education. Medical students use them for purposes
    such as supporting their self-directed learning. Educators are increasingly using them to support teaching, for example in simulating patient
    conversations. Adoption is generally ahead of institutional endorsement or training. OBJECTIVE This review seeks to better understand how and why
    medical students use LLMs and their educational impact. METHODS A systematic review was conducted, following PRISMA guidelines, of studies that
    involved medical students using LLMs and reported students’ use, perceptions or educational outcomes. Original peer-reviewed research available in
    English was included between November 2022 and July 2025. Two reviewers independently screened papers, extracted data and appraised quality using
    CASP. Data were synthesised using a narrative synthesis framework. RESULTS 54 studies were included, including cross-sectional and interventional
    studies. Awareness of LLMs was very high amongst medical students, while use was more variable. Most students used ChatGPT. Students report LLMs
    provided benefits to learning efficiency and more personalised learning, though this did not necessarily translate to better knowledge retention.
    Interventional studies using LLMs to simulate patient interactions, and provide personalised feedback, improved students’ performance and
    confidence. Students had concerns around accuracy, data, and overreliance which coexisted with use. Verification of outputs was often low but
    correlated with prior understanding of AI. Students were supportive of more formal training on AI. CONCLUSIONS LLMs can be useful in supporting
    students’ learning and are being adopted by medical students. They can provide benefits as tools for low-stakes practice of communication skills
    and in providing feedback. Many students have concerns around accuracy but lack the skills to critically appraise LLM outputs. Institutions should
    provide formal training on LLM use to develop verification habits, model good LLM-use, and prepare students for AI they will encounter in clinical
    practice. CLINICALTRIAL Badakhshan U, Ahadi N, Athavale R. Large language model use among medical students in medical education: a systematic
    review [protocol]. Open Science Framework; 2025. doi:10.17605/OSF.IO/YMCW8.
