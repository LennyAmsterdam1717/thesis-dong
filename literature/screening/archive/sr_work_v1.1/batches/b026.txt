TI: Financial Stability Implications of Generative AI: Taming the Animal Spirits
AB: This paper investigates the impact of the adoption of generative AI on financial stability. We conduct laboratory-style experiments using large
    language models to replicate classic studies on herd behavior in investment decisions. Our results show that AI agents make more rational
    decisions than humans, relying predominantly on private information over market trends. Increased reliance on AI-powered investment advice could
    therefore potentially lead to fewer asset price bubbles arising from animal spirits that trade by following the herd. However, exploring
    variations in the experimental settings reveals that AI agents can be induced to herd optimally when explicitly guided to make profit-maximizing
    decisions. While optimal herding improves market discipline, this behavior still carries potential implications for financial stability. In other
    experimental variations, we show that AI agents are not purely algorithmic, but have inherited some elements of human conditioning and bias.

TI: Prompt engineering as cognitive scaffolding for ethical and explanatory quality in AI-mediated financial learning
AB: Abstract Background Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in personal finance education, yet the role of instructional prompt design
    in shaping explanatory quality and ethical responsiveness remains underexplored. This matters because trustworthy financial guidance and
    instruction depend on clarity, accuracy, and appropriately cautionary framing. Method We conducted a Design-Based Research (DBR) Phase 1
    artefact-validation study using content analysis of 120 GPT-4-turbo (May 2025) generations. Ninety diagnostic prompts varied linguistic framing,
    cognitive level, and ethical intent; thirty Phase 1b prompts instantiated a derived taxonomy. Two expert reviewers scored outputs (0–3) for
    financial accuracy, conceptual clarity, and ethical tone (inter-rater reliability: $$\kappa =0.82$$ ). Results Prompt form was a pedagogical
    determinant of content quality. Procedural prompts outperformed interrogatives on clarity ( $$U=418$$ , $$p<.001$$ , $$r=.42$$ ) and accuracy (
    $$U=457$$ , $$p<.01$$ , $$r=.31$$ ). Ethical anchoring raised ethical tone (median 2.78 vs. 2.31; Cliff’s $$\delta =.46$$ , $$p<.001$$ ) with a
    modest accuracy trade-off ( $$\delta =-.18$$ , $$p=.04$$ ). Taxonomy-guided prompting increased medians for clarity ( $$+0.46$$ to 2.92), accuracy
    ( $$+0.41$$ to 2.81), and ethical tone ( $$+0.37$$ to 2.71), and reduced low-quality outliers from $$18\%$$ to $$4\%$$ ( $$\chi ^2=7.91$$ ,
    $$p=.005$$ ), evidencing higher instructional reliability at the content level. Conclusion The prompt taxonomy, validated here at the content
    level, operationalises three design principles - Guided Explanation, Contextualised Inquiry, and Comparative Reasoning. These links prompt forms
    to instructional functions via structural scaffolding and situated relevance. Treating prompt engineering as instructional design can
    systematically improve AI-mediated educational content in high-stakes finance. DBR Phase 2 will test impacts on learner outcomes (decision
    quality, self-regulation, calibrated trust) and robustness across models and retrieval-augmented settings.

AB: Artificial intelligence (AI) has moved from a back-office efficiency tool to a component of the decision architecture through which investments
    are researched, recommended, and executed. This review synthesises contemporary academic and authoritative institutional evidence on how
    AI-enabled financial technology (FinTech) reshapes investment decision-making, and on the behavioural, methodological, and regulatory conditions
    that determine whether that reshaping improves outcomes. The study adopts an integrative, thematic review approach, drawing on peer-reviewed
    studies, working papers, and primary regulatory and industry documents published predominantly between 2018 and 2026. Evidence is organised around
    five application streams—robo-advisory, machine and deep learning for prediction and portfolio construction, natural-language processing (NLP) and
    sentiment analytics, generative AI and large language models (LLMs), and the behavioural adoption of algorithmic advice—and is then read against a
    cross-cutting governance and risk layer. AI systems demonstrably widen the information set, automate signal generation, and mitigate several
    documented investor biases, and field evidence links robo-advice to higher equity participation, improved diversification, and better
    risk-adjusted returns, particularly for smaller and less sophisticated investors. Yet gains are uneven and conditional: statistical predictive
    superiority does not always translate into portfolio gains, algorithm aversion suppresses adoption for large investor segments, and opacity,
    herding, data-quality, and accountability risks are recognised across regulators. Explainability and hybrid human–AI designs recur as the pivotal
    moderators of trust. For practitioners and policymakers, value from AI-enabled FinTech depends less on model sophistication alone than on
    transparency, human oversight, data governance, and calibrated trust. The review consolidates an integrative framework and a research agenda for
    the human–AI hybrid investment decision system.

TI: Determinants of individual investors’ decisions to invest in digital assets: Evidence from Vietnam
AB: The rapid digitalization of financial markets has given rise to digital assets, yet individual investors' decision-making processes in emerging
    economies remain poorly understood. This study investigates the determinants influencing individual investors' intention to invest in digital
    assets (IIA) in Vietnam. Drawing upon the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), we propose an integrated
    model incorporating psychological, social, and technological constructs. Using a cross-sectional survey of 719 Vietnamese investors and Partial
    Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), we find that Perceived Behavioral Control (PBC; β = 0.487, p < 0.001) is the strongest

    (PR) positively influences IIA (β = 0.146, p < 0.001), suggesting risk-seeking behavior in this context, while Digital Financial Literacy (DFL)
    has a negative effect (β = −0.113, p = 0.009), indicating that greater knowledge may increase caution. Mediation analyses reveal that PR and
    Financial Risk Tolerance (FRT) play critical partial mediating roles. The model explains 58.8% of the variance in IIA and demonstrates robust
    predictive validity (Q 2 _predict >0). The study theoretically extends TPB/TAM to the digital asset context and practically offers insights for
    regulators, fintech platforms, and educators. We acknowledge the model's relatively simple structure and limited contextual scope; future research
    should integrate more dynamic, real-time data and explore the potential of Large Language Models (LLMs) for more nuanced behavioral analysis.

TI: Concepts in Context: The Large Concepts Model (LCM) and the Future of Neuro-Symbolic AI
AB: While modern AI has successfully entrenched itself across the workplace as a general-purpose assistant, its deployment in high-stakes domains –
    such as scientific analysis, engineering, medical research, and financial planning – is continually undermined by a lack of deterministic accuracy
    and structural analogical reasoning. As organizations push beyond the limits of probabilistic approximations and mandatory error disclaimers, the
    demand for rigorous, verifiable intelligence is paramount. Recent industry efforts have made significant strides toward these goals by introducing
    agentic workflows and stateful "System 1 / System 2" logic. These architectures excel at iterative, test-break-fix learning loops, functioning
    somewhat like cellular automata to empirically evaluate and restructure attempts until a solution is found. However, while these approaches
    improve multi-step execution, they neglect a crucial pillar of advanced cognition: analogical reasoning. Establishing fluid analogies was one of
    the foundational goals of early AI research, yet it remains profoundly undeveloped in modern applications. A robust cognitive system must possess
    the ability to apply structural analogies to navigate novel problems based on verified precedents – for example, recognizing that the harmonic
    resonance of a pendulum structurally mirrors a mass-spring system or an electronic resonator, and using that isomorphism to theorize the mechanics
    of ultracold molecular resonances, resonance-assisted quantum tunneling, or biological vibrational resonances. To bridge this gap, we propose the
    Large Concepts Model (LCM): a deterministic, neuro-symbolic architecture heavily guided by the active-symbol and mental-fluidity frameworks of
    Douglas Hofstadter and Melanie Mitchell, the stateful world-model paradigm of Yann LeCun, and the dual-process (System 1 / System 2) cognitive
    orchestration described by Demis Hassabis. The LCM strictly grounds the linguistic fluency of Large Language Models (LLMs) by operating on a
    decentralized "Library and Swarm of Librarians" paradigm rather than treating the LLM as a monolithic reasoning engine. An autonomous,
    self-healing concept graph acts as a highly structured, deterministic Library housing verified facts, structural ontologies, and executable
    mathematics. LLMs are operationalized strictly as a specialized swarm of librarians that curate, catalog, and navigate this knowledge. By routing
    decision-making through a cognitive state machine and multi-dimensional feature vectors – and by tracking emergent conceptual co-occurrences – the
    LCM transcends the brittle heuristics of standard Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). It provides not only the absolute accuracy required for
    zero-tolerance environments but also the verifiable analogical reasoning necessary to drive genuine scientific and engineering discovery.

TI: Fail-Closed AI: Why Architectural Guarantees Are Required for Regulated Deployment
AB: Large language models cannot be deployed in regulated domains because no existing approach can guarantee policy compliance. A single
    violation—investment advice without registration, medical diagnosis without licensure, legal advice without bar admission—triggers regulatory
    enforcement with severe consequences. Current approaches rely on probabilistic compliance through model training or post-hoc filtering. These
    cannot provide the deterministic guarantees required by regulatory frameworks. We argue that architectural enforcement, not model training, is the
    necessary foundation for regulated AI deployment. By reconceptualizing execution itself as the enforcement mechanism, systems can provide
    guarantees that violations cannot reach users regardless of model behavior. This paper examines why probabilistic approaches are structurally
    insufficient, what properties an architectural solution must satisfy, and why this represents a fundamental shift in how AI safety is
    conceptualized. The core insight: execution completion should be conditional on validity, not optimistic. Systems that fail to generate compliant
    output should fail to generate any output. This fail-closed principle—borrowed from safety-critical engineering—provides the missing foundation
    for trustworthy AI in high-stakes domains.
